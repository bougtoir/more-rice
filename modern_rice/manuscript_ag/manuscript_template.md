# TITLE
Where could rice cultivation systems be transferred beyond traditional growing regions? A multi-scale gate analysis of thermal transfer, water access and food-supply risk on European arable land

# ABSTRACT
Proposals to grow rice outside its traditional regions often treat a climatic threshold as evidence of suitability. We test that inference geographically for {inc_Mha} Mha of incumbent arable land in Europe (SPAM 2010 harvested area of wheat, maize, barley and potato, 5′ grid) using five sequential, non-compensatory gates: thermal transfer of a cold-region japonica system, irrigation infrastructure, rainfed water balance, economic parity and food-supply risk. Calibrating the Hokkaido cold bound on the same WorldClim grid gives {T_star} GDD10 (5th percentile of Hokkaido rice area), not the ~900 GDD10 obtained from NASA POWER point cells; the thermal gate is therefore met by {pct_thermal_pass}% of incumbent arable land, whereas a transplanted 900 threshold would retain {pct_pass_900}% and almost exclude Germany ({de_T}% vs {de_900}%). Water, not temperature, is the binding constraint: {pct_aei}% of incumbent arable land combines thermal compatibility with irrigation infrastructure (area equipped ≥10%), while {pct_deficit}% faces a positive Jun–Sep rainfed deficit. Point-scale daily water balances agree with the grid deficit (Spearman ρ = {rho}). On the Lower Danube, a 250,000 ha paddy scenario would require ~{dan_q} m³/s, about {dan_pct}% of median observed summer low flow; allocation, not discharge, is unresolved. Correcting an analog-yield inconsistency and adding block-bootstrap intervals, modeled shortfall-probability reductions have bootstrap intervals that exclude zero in {kz_excl}% of sensitivity configurations for Kazakhstan and {ro_excl}% for Romania (baseline 95% CI {ro_lo} to {ro_hi}), and are not robust elsewhere. The results identify a small set of irrigated-infrastructure regions for next-stage feasibility assessment, not land allocations or implemented irrigation.

# 1. Introduction
Diversifying staple crops is promoted as a response to correlated harvest failures, because temperature and precipitation anomalies co-move across breadbaskets and the probability of simultaneous failure is changing (Gaupp et al., 2020; Mehrabi & Ramankutty, 2019; Ray et al., 2015). Diversity can stabilise national production (Renard & Tilman, 2019), but adding a crop reduces risk only where the crop can actually be grown, where its resource demands can be met, and where its failures are not synchronous with those of incumbent crops. Each of these conditions has a geography, and each depends on scale.

Rice is a demanding test case. It is the most irrigated staple, it carries high caloric yields per hectare where it succeeds, and its cultivation has expanded at cold margins. In Northeast China, paddy expansion under warming has been documented and attributed in part to climate change (Gao & Liu, 2011; Liu et al., 2022), and the potential northern limits of particular rice systems have been mapped from heat-accumulation thresholds (Yang et al., 2023). Global suitability assessments also map where rice could be grown (Zabel et al., 2014). Applied land-suitability work, however, shows that suitability maps are sensitive to criteria, weights and thresholds (Xu & Zhang, 2013; Tenerelli & Carver, 2012). It also shows that irrigation-led expansion can conflict with basin water sustainability (Nouri et al., 2017; Saiko & Zonn, 2000). Water governance, rather than climate, often shapes how rice systems adapt (Burchfield & Gilligan, 2016).

Three inferential shortcuts recur when rice transfer is discussed outside traditional regions. First, a thermal threshold taken from one data source and one place is applied to another, as if it were a property of the crop rather than of a cultivation system measured at a particular scale. Second, abundant river discharge is read as available irrigation water. Third, modeled reductions in a scenario's shortfall probability are read as resilience gains. This study treats all three as geographic questions and asks a narrower question than suitability mapping: on existing arable land in Europe, where does a transferable rice cultivation system pass successive biophysical and infrastructural gates? Where does it fail? And how do these answers change with data source, seasonal window, spatial resolution and aggregation? A further question is whether rice substitution changes modeled food-supply risk differently from a non-rice comparator in the countries that pass.

We make three contributions. (i) We separate thermal transfer, irrigation infrastructure and rainfed water balance as non-compensatory gates and map them with an explicit land denominator, the harvested area of incumbent arable crops. Results are therefore exposures of existing land use to each gate, not convertible areas. (ii) We show that the commonly quoted cold-region bound is source- and scale-specific. Calibrated consistently on one grid, it stops being the binding constraint, and the binding constraint moves to water and infrastructure. (iii) We re-evaluate the mainstem water and portfolio-risk arguments for the Lower Danube and other candidates with corrected calculations and uncertainty intervals. # 2. Data and methods
## 2.1 Study region, spatial unit and denominator
The study domain is Europe as delineated by Natural Earth 1:50 m country boundaries (Natural Earth, 2024), excluding Russia, whose territory is truncated by the 45°E edge of the 11°W–45°E, 34°N–71.5°N processing window. The spatial unit is the 5′ grid cell (about 9.3 km at the equator). The denominator is the 2010 harvested area of wheat, maize, barley and potato in the Spatial Production Allocation Model, all technologies combined (IFPRI, 2019; Yu et al., 2020). We call this incumbent arable land. It totals {inc_Mha} Mha in the domain. Because the processing window also contains North Africa, Anatolia and the Caucasus ({rect_Mha} Mha of incumbent arable land in total), all European shares are computed with an explicit country mask; Section 3.2 reports how the shares change with the domain definition. It is a measure of where arable cropping already occurs and where rice would compete with incumbent crops; it is not an estimate of land that could or should be converted. Countries are assigned by rasterising the same boundaries at 5′. The data sources are listed in Table 1.

## 2.2 Thermal transfer gate
Commercial rice production in Hokkaido relies on ultra-early japonica cultivars, heated nurseries, transplanting and warm-water irrigation (MAFF, 2024). We use Hokkaido as the reference for the transferable cold-region system, not as a universal physiological threshold. Thermal accumulation is GDD10, the sum of the daily mean temperature above 10 °C. In the point analysis, NASA POWER daily data for 2000–2024 (NASA POWER Project, 2025; evaluated by Bai et al., 2010) give GDD10 over the season window used by the original pipeline. Because that window was computed from a pseudo day-of-year (month × 30.4 + day), it actually runs from about 29 April to 11 September rather than June to September; we report both windows and a May–September window. On the grid, GDD10 is computed from WorldClim 2.1 monthly means for 1970–2000 (Fick & Hijmans, 2017) at 2.5′, 5′ and 10′ resolution, weighting each month by the number of its days that fall in the window. The grid threshold T* is calibrated on the same grid: it is the 5th percentile of GDD10 across Hokkaido rice cells, weighted by SPAM 2010 rice harvested area ({hok_rice_kha} thousand ha). It is reported alongside the 1st, 10th and 50th percentiles and the 1st and 5th percentiles of all rice north of 35°N.

## 2.3 Water gates
Irrigation infrastructure is represented by the area equipped for irrigation in the Global Map of Irrigation Areas version 5 (Siebert et al., 2013), expressed as a share of cell area. Cells with a share of at least 10% are classed as having existing infrastructure. This is a conservative indicator of infrastructure presence, not of water allocated to rice. For rainfed systems we compute a climatic balance on the grid, D = Σ (Kc·ET0 − P) over June–September, using Hargreaves–Samani reference evapotranspiration (Hargreaves & Samani, 1985) from WorldClim 10′ monthly minimum and maximum temperature, extraterrestrial radiation, and the Kc schedule of the point model (1.05–1.20; Allen et al., 1998). At the point cells, a daily single-layer root-zone water balance (total available water 100 mm, stress at 30% depletion, 120-day season) gives the share of years with more than ten deficit days during flowering. The two measures are compared by rank correlation.

For the Lower Danube, the continuous-equivalent demand Q = A·D/T of a paddy scenario is compared with observed summer low flows at Orsova (GRDC station 6742200, immediately downstream of the Iron Gates I dam; Drobeta-Turnu Severin until 1971). Daily discharge is not openly available, so we use indices derived from GRDC daily means in EStreams v1.4 (do Nascimento et al., 2024): monthly minima and means of daily discharge for 1920–1990, converted from specific discharge with the official catchment area (576,232 km²). For each of the {dan_lf_n} years with complete June–September records we take the minimum daily discharge in June–September; we report the median and 10th percentile across years with year-level bootstrap intervals, and the Jun–Sep mean flow. This is a seasonal minimum, not a flow-duration Q99, which would require the daily series. Bazias (upstream of the reservoir, 1991–2008) is used as a sensitivity check. We use seasonal depths of 800–1,200 mm and seasons of 120–150 days (Bouman et al., 2007), and draw on a water-allocation model of the Iron Gates–Zimnicea reach (Salam et al., 2022). Q is a net field requirement and a seasonal average rate, not a peak abstraction; with a conveyance efficiency of 0.75 the gross abstraction would be {dan_q_gross} m³/s, and return flows would partly offset it downstream. River discharge is not allocable irrigation water: environmental flows, navigation, hydropower and existing abstractions have prior claims, and the paddy demand would be located on tributaries and canal commands rather than at the gauge. This is a mainstem comparison only; it does not establish water rights, conveyance or farm-level access.

## 2.4 Gate cascade and validation
Cells with incumbent arable area are assigned to one of four classes: (1) thermal gate not met; (2) thermal gate met, no infrastructure, and D > 0; (3) thermal gate met, no infrastructure, and D ≤ 0; (4) thermal gate met with infrastructure. Areas are summed by country. As an observational check, we overlay the same classes on the SPAM 2010 rice harvested area that already exists in Europe ({eu_rice_kha} thousand ha).

## 2.5 Scale, window and source sensitivity
To assess the modifiable areal unit problem (Jelinski & Wu, 1996), the share of incumbent arable land passing thresholds T ∈ {T*, 800, 900, 1,000, 1,100} is computed at native 2.5′, 5′ and 10′ resolution and after aggregating the 5′ GDD10 field to 30′ and 60′ block means. For each configuration we report the area disagreement with the 2.5′ result. We also compare NASA POWER point values with the WorldClim 5′ cell that contains each point.

## 2.6 Economic parity
Observed temperate paddy costs exist in the audited sources only for Italy: total costs {it_cost} €/ha and gross margins {it_gm} €/ha for conventional systems (Savoini et al., 2025; Monaco et al., 2016). Rice-specific costs for Romania, Bulgaria, Croatia and Serbia were not found in public sources. Economics is therefore treated as a break-even condition: rice must match the incumbent margin per hectare. It is not treated as an observed regional profitability.

## 2.7 Food-supply risk scenarios
For each country, FAOSTAT yields (FAO, 2024) of wheat, barley, maize, rice and potato are log-detrended, and the residuals are used as yearly shocks to recent yield levels. The incumbent portfolio (A) is compared with a substitution of 20% of the incumbent area by rice (B) and by potato (D). The outcome is the modeled probability that portfolio calories fall below 80% of their mean. FAOSTAT rice yields refer to paddy and are converted to milled equivalent (factor 0.67) before applying the energy density of milled rice. Where a country has fewer than 15 years of rice yields, rice shocks and the rice yield level are both taken from an analog country. The original implementation combined analog shocks with a missing own-country rice level, which set the rice contribution to zero; we correct this and label such countries as analog evidence. Uncertainty in B − A is estimated by a moving-block bootstrap with 3-year blocks and 2,000 resamples (Künsch, 1989). Robustness is assessed over 24 configurations: substitution shares of 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3; linear or quadratic detrending; a rice yield scale of 1.0 or 0.75; and own-mean or baseline-mean thresholds. These are scenario statistics about modeled calorie variability, not estimates of observed food crises.

# 3. Results
## 3.1 The cold-region bound is source- and window-specific
At the NASA POWER point cell, Hokkaido accumulates {hok_pipe} GDD10 in the pipeline window, {hok_cal} in calendar June–September and {hok_may} in May–September. The ranking of European candidates against Hokkaido depends on the window. Under the pipeline window, Rhine ({rh_pipe}) and Saxony ({sx_pipe}) exceed the Hokkaido value. Under calendar June–September they fall just below it ({rh_cal} and {sx_cal} vs {hok_cal}), because Hokkaido's late season is relatively warm. Alsace, Loire and Berlin remain above Hokkaido in all three windows.

On the WorldClim grid, rice-area-weighted GDD10 across Hokkaido rice cells has a 5th percentile of {T_star} and a median of {hok_p50} (1st and 10th percentiles {hok_p01} and {hok_p10}). The point-derived ~900 bound therefore lies near the median of the grid distribution, not at its cold tail. Across 33 European point cells, NASA POWER (2000–2024) exceeds the containing WorldClim cell (1970–2000) by a median of {pg_median} GDD10 (range {pg_min} to {pg_max}). The difference reflects the warmer recent period and also the coarse 0.5° POWER grid, which smooths terrain. The Alsace point, for example, falls in a Vosges cell at 5′ ({als_grid} vs {als_power}). A threshold is thus meaningful only when it is calibrated in the data and at the scale where it is applied.

## 3.2 Gate cascade on incumbent arable land
With the grid-consistent threshold, the thermal gate is met on {pct_thermal_pass}% of incumbent arable land ({thermal_pass_Mha} Mha) (Figure 2a; Table 2). Thermal failure is concentrated in Ireland, Scotland, Fennoscandia, the Baltic states and uplands. Most thermally compatible land, {deficit_Mha} Mha ({pct_deficit}%), lacks irrigation infrastructure and faces a positive rainfed deficit. Only {bal_kha} thousand ha combine no infrastructure with a non-positive deficit. Thermal compatibility combined with existing infrastructure covers {aei_Mha} Mha ({pct_aei}%) and is concentrated in Spain, Italy, Romania, France, Ukraine, Bulgaria and Hungary (Figure 2b). Serbia and Croatia have almost no incumbent arable land in this class. The infrastructure class depends on the equipped-area cut-off: with cut-offs of 5% and 20%, it covers {pct_aei5}% and {pct_aei20}% of incumbent arable land, and {rice_aei5}% and {rice_aei20}% of existing rice.

## 3.3 Threshold, window and resolution sensitivity
The share of incumbent arable land passing the thermal gate falls steeply with the threshold (Figure 3a): from {pct_thermal_pass}% at T* to {pct_pass_900}% at 900 and {pct_pass_1000}% at 1,000 GDD10. The national implications are larger than the Europe-wide totals suggest. Germany passes on {de_T}% of its incumbent arable land at T* but on only {de_900}% at 900. Using the calendar June–September window and its own calibrated threshold ({T_star_cal}) gives {pct_thermal_cal}%, and the two windows classify {pct_agree}% of the area identically. Changing the resolution from 2.5′ to 10′, or aggregating to 30′ or 60′, changes the Europe-wide share by at most about {max_scale_pts} percentage points (Figure 3b). The area that changes class is nevertheless substantial: {dis_900_60} Mha at 900 GDD10 when 60′ is compared with 2.5′. Nationally, Romania's passing share at 900 falls from {ro_900_25}% at 2.5′ to {ro_900_60}% at 60′. Classification near the bound is therefore resolution-dependent even where continental totals are stable. The source cross-walk (Figure 3c) shows the systematic offset between point and grid data.

## 3.4 Water geography
The rainfed deficit is the gate that rice cannot bypass in continental Europe. The grid deficit D and the point-scale frequency of flowering-stage deficits are concordant (Spearman ρ = {rho}, p = {rho_p}, n = {rho_n}; Figure 4a). Flowering deficits occur in 88–100% of years at continental cells and in 64–84% at Nordic and Baltic cells, compared with {hok_flower}% in Hokkaido. A rainfed upland system would therefore share the drought exposure of incumbent cereals where diversification is most needed.

For irrigated lowland rice on the Lower Danube, the mainstem comparison scales with area (Figure 4b). The median June–September minimum daily discharge at Orsova was {dan_lf_med} m³/s (95% CI {dan_lf_lo}–{dan_lf_hi}; 10th percentile {dan_lf_p10} m³/s; lowest {dan_lf_low} m³/s), and the median June–September mean flow {dan_mean} m³/s. A 100,000 ha scenario at 1,000 mm over 120 days requires {dan_q_100} m³/s ({dan_pct_100}% of the median seasonal minimum). A 250,000 ha scenario requires {dan_q} m³/s net, {dan_pct}% of the median seasonal minimum and {dan_pct_p10}% of its 10th percentile; the gross abstraction of {dan_q_gross} m³/s is {dan_pct_gross}% of the median and {dan_pct_gross_low}% of the lowest observed seasonal minimum. Gross abstraction exceeded 10% of the seasonal minimum in {dan_yrs10}% of years and 20% in {dan_yrs20}%. After the Iron Gates I dam (1972–1990) the 10th percentile fell to {dan_lf_reg_p10} m³/s. These ratios compare a hypothetical demand with a river statistic; they do not imply that this share of the low flow is available for irrigation. The allocation model for the Iron Gates–Zimnicea reach reports no agricultural deficit under its scenarios (Salam et al., 2022), but it does not represent a new paddy sector of this size, and the record ends in 1990. Conveyance, pumping, water rights, environmental flows and transboundary arrangements therefore remain the decisive unknowns.

## 3.5 Food-supply risk
After correction (Figure 5; Table 3), substituting 20% of the incumbent area with rice lowers the modeled shortfall probability in Romania from {ro_A} to {ro_B} (potato comparator {ro_D}). The 95% block-bootstrap interval of the change is {ro_lo} to {ro_hi}. B is below A in {ro_rob}% of the 24 configurations, but the interval excludes zero in only {ro_excl}%. In Kazakhstan the reduction ({kz_A} to {kz_B}) has an interval that excludes zero ({kz_lo} to {kz_hi}) in {kz_excl}% of configurations. For Serbia, the earlier contrast between rice (0.211) and potato (0.053) was an artifact of the analog-yield inconsistency. The corrected estimate ({rs_B} vs potato {rs_D}) rests on Hungarian rice shocks and is analog evidence only. In Bulgaria, Hungary and France, the direction of the effect is not robust across configurations (B < A in {bg_rob}%, {hu_rob}% and {fr_rob}% of configurations, respectively).

## 3.6 Economic parity
Observed Italian costs and margins bound what a temperate paddy system must earn. Without observed costs in the candidate countries, the analysis can state only the parity condition. Rice is economically comparable where its gross margin per hectare, net of irrigation and drying costs, at least matches that of the wheat or maize it would replace. This condition is evaluated nowhere in this study and must be measured in field trials.

# 4. Discussion
## 4.1 What changes with geography, scale and constraint
The main result concerns the location of the binding constraint. When a cold-region bound measured at point cells is transplanted to a gridded climatology, temperature appears to exclude much of central and northern Europe. When the bound is calibrated on the grid where it is applied, temperature is binding only at the Atlantic and northern margins and in uplands, and the geography of feasibility follows irrigation infrastructure. This reversal is a scale and data-source effect of the kind that land-suitability studies warn about (Jelinski & Wu, 1996; Xu & Zhang, 2013). It matters here because it changes which regions merit attention. Rhine, Saxony, Berlin and Alsace were previously advanced as cold-region transfer candidates. They are thermally compatible, but almost all of their incumbent arable land lacks infrastructure and faces rainfed deficits. Conversely, the regions where thermal compatibility and infrastructure coincide are those that already grow rice or have irrigated cropping: northern Italy, Spain, southern France, the Lower Danube in Romania and Bulgaria, and Hungary. The screen is consistent with the observed distribution of European rice.

## 4.2 Relation to previous work
Compared with threshold-based mapping of rice limits (Yang et al., 2023) and global suitability (Zabel et al., 2014), the present analysis contributes three things: a land denominator, separation of non-compensatory gates, and explicit sensitivity to source, window and resolution. Compared with observed cold-margin expansion in Northeast China (Gao & Liu, 2011; Liu et al., 2022), it does not claim that Europe will follow a similar trajectory. That expansion occurred within an existing paddy culture and with water development. Evidence from the Aral basin and Lake Urmia (Saiko & Zonn, 2000; Nouri et al., 2017) is a reminder that irrigation expansion can be the problem rather than the solution, so the water gate cannot be treated as a formality. The portfolio results agree with studies showing that diversification stabilises production on average (Renard & Tilman, 2019), but they also show how fragile country-specific scenario gains are once implementation and resampling uncertainty are included.

## 4.3 Candidates for next-stage feasibility assessment
Only cells that meet the thermal gate and have existing irrigation infrastructure are candidates for further assessment. Among the countries examined, the Lower Danube in Romania combines thermal compatibility, infrastructure, a large mainstem flow and a modeled risk reduction whose direction is consistent across configurations, although its intervals mostly include zero. It is therefore a candidate for targeted feasibility assessment or field trials, and not an implementation-ready region. Such an assessment would need to establish allocable water at the farm level, conveyance and pumping costs, local rice production costs and margins, cultivar performance, and the methane and nitrogen implications of flooded rice. Bulgaria and Hungary have infrastructure but non-robust risk effects. Serbia and Croatia lack infrastructure on incumbent arable land and rest on analog risk evidence.

## 4.4 Limitations
The climate layers differ in period (WorldClim 1970–2000; POWER 2000–2024), and monthly GDD underestimates daily accumulation near the 10 °C base. Computed from the same daily POWER series, the monthly approximation is {hok_mbias} GDD10 lower than daily accumulation at the Hokkaido point in the pipeline window and between {eu_mbias_min} and {eu_mbias_max} GDD10 at the European points, so it makes the grid bound slightly conservative. Calibrating within each source addresses this but does not remove it. SPAM 2010 and GMIA v5 are circa-2010 products and are subject to their own disaggregation errors. The equipped-area share indicates the presence of infrastructure, not water allocated to rice. The thermal bound represents one cultivation system and does not address cold-water damage at booting or the availability of cultivars. Gate classes describe the exposure of existing arable land and are not an optimisation of land allocation. They imply no converted area, observed land-use change or causal policy effect. Costs outside Italy are unobserved. The risk analysis is national and calorie-based, ignores trade and storage, and treats rice shocks from short or analog series as weak evidence. Future climate was not modeled.

# 5. Conclusions
Whether rice can be transferred beyond its traditional regions is a geographic question whose answer depends on the data source, window and resolution used to define a cultivation-system bound. Calibrated consistently, the thermal requirement of a cold-region japonica system is met on most European arable land. Irrigation infrastructure and allocable water are the binding constraints. Mainstem flow arguments understate the scale of the allocation problem, and modeled supply-risk gains are rarely distinguishable from zero. Land-use assessment should therefore move from asking whether rice can grow to asking where infrastructure, water rights and costs allow a functioning rice system, and it should begin with targeted feasibility studies in the few irrigated regions that pass every mapped gate.
