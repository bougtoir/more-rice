## A1 — Llibre del Repartiment rice/arrozales entries

**Verdict: INACCESSIBLE at folio level (PARTIAL at document level)**

- What was searched: the UJI *Arxiu Jaume I* diplomatic transcription of the Llibre del Repartiment
  (jaumeprimer.uji.es, entries 1–664 — corresponding to ACA Real Cancillería **Registro 5**,
  *Donationum regni Valentie*, Jul 1237–Jun 1245 + 1252 supplement). Raw capture preserved at
  `data/raw/jaumeprimer_llibre_repartiment_reg5_entries1-664_20261025.html` (SHA-256 in ledger).
- Result: **zero** occurrences of *arroz / arròs / arrossal / arrossar / oryza* in the 664 entries.
  Only unrelated homographs (Roçafa, Rossel, rossilló place-names, one personal name Avenambroz).
- Consequence 1: the alleged rice-field entries are **not in Registro 5**. If they exist they are in
  **Registros 6–7** (local repartimiento notebooks, 1247–1249 and later), which are not transcribed online.
- Consequence 2: secondary literature asserts the book "ya describe arrozales" (DO Arroz de Valencia pliego;
  AHDE 2022) **without folio citation** — Phase 2's claim of an "explicitly registered arrozales" must be
  downgraded from *verified primary evidence (grade C)* to *attested by secondary literature only*.
- Confidence: HIGH that no arroz entry exists in Reg. 5 transcription; UNVERIFIED for Reg. 6–7.
- Paper impact: the Valencia continuity case now rests on (a) Furs/cartas puebla water clauses (A–B,
  verified), (b) secondary attestation of arrozales in Reg. 6–7 (unverified folio), (c) archaeobotanical/
  documentary literature. A human-in-archive PARES check of Reg. 6–7 remains the single blocking task for
  any manuscript making the "earliest surviving rice-field register" claim. **Removed from load-bearing use.**

## A2 — Orihuela/Murcia repartimiento rice silence

**Verdict: INACCESSIBLE (direct examination impossible remotely)**

- The *Llibre del Repartiment de Orihuela* is an 87-folio codex of six partitions, 1266–1314, five Castilian
  + one Catalan section, redacted c. 1310 (Wikipedia/ACA description; Dialnet 58712 linguistic study).
- No digitized full transcription was found online. Search of scholarly literature confirms the codex exists
  and is heavily studied for onomastics/language contact, but **no study located reports any rice entry** —
  and none was located *denying* one either.
- **Per guardrail 3, the prior "Orihuela silent on rice" entry is recoded as D0** (absence of accessible
  evidence), NOT evidence of absent cultivation. The counter-case remains methodologically valid only as a
  *documented-evidence asymmetry*, not as a cultivation discontinuity.
- Confidence: the repartimiento text itself UNVERIFIED; the *absence of a reported rice mention in the
  scholarly literature on that codex* is verified.
- Paper impact: the Valencia-vs-Orihuela contrast survives only as a **documentation asymmetry contrast**
  (one archive's transcribed register attests continuity claims; the other's published scholarship is silent),
  not as a cultivation discontinuity. Cannot carry causal weight.

## A3 — Statute of Cambridge 1388 text

**Verdict: PASS**

- 12 Ric. II c. 7 (17 October 1388), verified in Ruffhead, *Statutes at Large* vol. I (Wikisource scan
  Page:Ruffhead...djvu/426): labourers leaving their hundred/rape/wapentake "sanz lettre tesmoigniale"
  are treated as beggars able to serve — the testimonial/letter system is confirmed in statute text.
- Statute of Labourers 1351 (25 Edw. III) likewise concerns servants' departure from their hundred.
- Content is **mobility and status regulation only** — no provision touching crop choice, seed, or cultivation
  technique. Verified: a *labor-margin* regulation, exactly as Phase 2 claimed, and strictly post-period
  context (never contemporaneous evidence for 1000–1300).

## A4 — Dating of sanitary restrictions

**Verdict: PASS (with refinement)**

- Separated into two chronologies:
  - **Within 1000–1300:** conquest-era evidence only — malaria mortality among post-conquest settlers
    (1238 aftermath, attested in conquest chronicle/secondary literature) and early medical-theoretical
    linking of stagnant wetland water to *fiebres tercianas*. **No formal medieval prohibition located
    ≤1300.**
  - **Post-1300 (early modern):** formal cultivation prohibitions/restrictions — intermittently legislated
    15th–18th centuries, most intensely post-1609 (Morisco expulsion) and in the 18th–19th c. Albufera
    polemic.
- Consequence: the *sanitary motive* is documented as a *sentiment/practice* from the conquest period, but
  every *formal prohibition* is post-medieval. The reviewer disagreement is resolved by stating both facts
  separately; no post-1300 restriction is used to infer 1000–1300 motives (guardrail 1 honoured).
