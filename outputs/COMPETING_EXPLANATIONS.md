# COMPETING EXPLANATIONS — non-adoption of rice in medieval Western Europe (AD 1000–1300)

Assessment standard: which explanations are necessary and sufficient to explain the observed pattern *without* invoking political suppression (H3/H4)?

## Ranked by explanatory power

| # | Explanation | Evidence status | Verdict |
|---|-------------|-----------------|---------|
| 1 | **Climate / growing-season temperature** | Grain formation needs ~20 °C+ mean; flowering ~23 °C. July means: England ~16.5 °C, N. France ~18.5 °C, Germany ~18 °C — all below the grain-formation threshold. Madagascar upland trials (S16) show severe sterility at Tmin 13/Tmax 19 °C. Muthukumaran (S04): "rice was not suited for growth in northern Europe." | **Sufficient alone for NW Europe.** No political story needed. |
| 2 | **Irrigation infrastructure requirement** | Every documented medieval cultivation site (Guadalquivir marshes, Valencia huerta, later Po Valley) is *irrigated*. Building acequia networks requires capital and institutions Andalusi regimes had already built; absent north of Mediterranean littoral. | **Sufficient for Mediterranean fringe:** where rice could grow, it needed infrastructure only Muslim Iberia had. |
| 3 | **Knowledge transmission barrier** | Practical (K3) knowledge existed only in Arabic-language agronomy (Filāḥa corpus). Ibn al-Awwam had no Latin translation until Banqueri 1802. Latin Europe's knowledge ceiling was K1 (commodity)–K2 (anecdote). | **Sufficient complement to #1-#2.** Decision-makers literally could not have "chosen" upland rice — they lacked the cultivar-concept and methods. |
| 4 | **Seed availability** | Cultivation requires viable seed stock within reach. Documented seed transfers are Mediterranean: Valencia (internal), Sforza→Este gift 1475. No medieval seed channel into NW Europe is recorded. | Strong support for non-adoption; no suppression needed. |
| 5 | **Dietary preferences / market structure** | Rice = luxury import (Hanseatic Lenten/almond dishes, S10; elite cookbooks, S20); wheat/rye/oats anchored bread, pottage, brewing and the tithe/tax accounting systems. | Consistent with low pull-factor. |
| 6 | **Crop failure risk / path dependence** | A cultivar with zero local agronomic track record and a narrow thermal margin is rational to reject even without coercion. | Consistent. |
| 7 | **Labor requirements** | Wet-rice is labor-intensive (transplanting, weeding, water management). Under Domar-type logic (S14), scarce labor + labor-intensive crop is *bad* for landlord surplus — opposite of the suppression hypothesis's premise (the hypothesis needs rice to threaten labor control, not demand more labor). | Argues the posited mechanism is backwards. |

## Weaker / contested explanations

| Explanation | Assessment |
|-------------|------------|
| **Tithes & taxation** | Tithes attached to existing crops plausibly slow substitution — a real *institutional friction*, but a friction, not suppression. No rice-specific tithe record exists. |
| **Common-field institutions** | Open-field communality plausibly resists novel monocrops; applies to *any* innovation, not rice-specifically. |
| **Property rights / commons** | Relevant to irrigation investment (who owns the water), reinforces #2. |
| **Political control / peasant dependence (the audit hypothesis)** | **No documentary support anywhere** (P04, P05, P09). Where a ruler did act on rice (Sforza export ban, P03), the act protected *his* monopoly on the new high-yield crop — economically the opposite of suppressing productivity. The posited mechanism (lower output preserves dependence) contradicts observed landlord behavior (P06: Winchester lords tracked and raised yields) and Domar-type theory (P08: coercion binds *labor*, not *output*). |
| **Public health (malaria)** | **Now documented in-period**: Valencia restricted/prohibited paddies in marsh zones from the post-1238 conquest aftermath onward (intermittent, strongest 15th–18th c.), motive = 'fiebres tercianas' (P11, S21/S24/S25/S26). The genuine historical objection to paddies — sanitary, not dependence-motivated. |
| **Confessional stigma ("Muslim food")** | Post-conquest Valencia coded rice as Muslim food occupying land for "Christian products" (S25); Iberian impurity/commensality rhetoric (S22, S23) provides the discourse channel — the closest medieval analogue to the potato's "devil's root" stigma, again dependence-irrelevant. |

## Conclusion

Non-adoption in AD 1000–1300 Western Europe is overdetermined by climate (#1), infrastructure (#2) and knowledge transmission (#3). Political suppression is not required to explain any observation and is supported by no document. The hypothesis's causal mechanism is also internally suspect: landlords profiting from peasant *output* have no obvious reason to cap it, and the one documented ruler intervention on rice (1475 Milan) promoted it while restricting only its *export*.
