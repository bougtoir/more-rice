# Labor regulation vs crop regulation — the dependence-preserving mechanism that actually existed

## The documented machinery of coercion (post-Black Death)

| Instrument | Region | Date | Content | Motive (documented) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinance of Labourers | England (Edward III) | 1349 | Compulsory service for all able-bodied under 60 without land/craft; wages frozen at 1346 level; fines for refusing | Labor scarcity after plague; preserve customary wage/service relations |
| Statute of Labourers | England (Parliament) | 1351 | Same wage caps; prohibited workers leaving their "hundred" before term served; penalties on *both* workers and hiring lords; enforcement via sheriffs/lords | Explicit: workers demanding "double and treble" wages |
| Statute strengthening | England | 1360–61 | Fugitive laborers to be captured and imprisoned; later branding | Enforcement of the 1351 regime |
| Statute of Cambridge | England | 1388 | Travel permits: no laborer/servant may depart their hundred without a letter under the King's seal stating reason; beggars/pilgrims controlled | Internal-passport system for rural labor |
| Ordonnance | France (Jean II) | 1351 | Wage caps, compulsory work provisions mirroring the English statute | Same post-plague labor scarcity |
| Stadtluft macht frei (custom) | German territories | 12th–15th c. | Serf resident 1 year + 1 day in a privileged town becomes free | Counter-institution: towns as labor sinks |
| Vagrancy/poor laws (Tudor) | England | 1495–1601 | Punishment of "sturdy beggars"; settlement acts later fix the poor to parishes | Post-medieval evolution of mobility control |
| Sentencia de Guadalupe | Catalonia | 1486 | Crown abolishes remença serfdom for redemption payment — resolution via legal freeing, not suppression | Ending rural disorder; fiscal settlement |

## Why this is the mechanism the rice hypothesis was looking for

The audit hypothesis (H3/H4) asked whether rulers suppressed a high-productivity
crop to keep peasants dependent. What the record shows instead:

1. **The binding constraint was labor, not crops.** After 1348, scarcity of labor
   — not of land or output — was the threat to manorial revenue. The documented
   response was to bind the *worker*: wage ceilings, service compulsion, travel
   letters, fugitive recovery. No comparable corpus exists for crop choice.

2. **The intervention failed.** English real wages rose through the 15th century;
   villeinage decayed de facto before any formal abolition. Domar's model predicts
   exactly this: coercion is applied to labor (the scarce factor), and it is
   sustainable only while enforcement is cheap — depopulated post-plague England
   could not enforce it.

3. **Rice regulation never carried this motive.** Where rice *was* regulated
   (Valencia), the statutory motive was sanitary ('fiebres tercianas') and
   secondarily confessional; where it was promoted (Sforza, James I), rulers
   sought output and revenue. The labor-binding instruments (1351/1388) nowhere
   mention crop choice; the Valencia rice ordinances nowhere mention labor control.

4. **Po Valley coda (post-period).** Rice's actual north-European expansion
   (1475 onward) ran on *labor flexibility*: seasonal migrants into reclaimed
   marshland, paid in wages — the opposite of bound labor. The crop that needed
   the most labor coordination arrived precisely when and where labor was freest
   and most contractual.

## Bottom line

"Was productivity suppressed to preserve dependence?" mis-aims one level: in
medieval Europe, dependence was defended on the labor margin (mobility and wage
law), not the technology margin (crop choice). The evidence the hypothesis needs
— a regulation trading aggregate output for peasant immobility — does not exist
for rice and is unnecessary for the hypothesis's real subject, which is already
well documented under labor law.
